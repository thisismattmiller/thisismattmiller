Jeopardy! Video Games of the 80s and 90s

Analysis of 24K questions extracted

Aug 3 2026

I have been looking at trivia game stuff recently and also been thinking about ROM emulation files, for old console gaming systems like Nintendo, as a data corpus. And it made me remember seeing these Jeopardy games around growing up and thinking how awful they must be. But now that I’m old I kind of think they are neat, or at least conceptually. There ended up being quite a lot of games released over the ~10 years that cartridge systems were hugely popular. And I wondered what kind of questions were on these things? The market is of course mostly for children but the Jeopardy brand is pretty serious trivia. So I gathered them up and extracted the questions and did some analysis.

Getting the questions out I made use of a coding agent to search the binary ROM file. This is a good example of using them where it can just churn away brute forcing binary offsets of the 14 files for as long as needed. I did find a hint before I started that the early games used the classic Huffman coding algorithm so that was a good start:

You can see the early games were space restricted so there are more elaborate approaches to packing the questions into the cartridge. While at the end of the run, the tens of megabytes the N64 allows for storage enabled luxurious plain ASCII text.

There were 34K questions extracted total but if you remove duplicates across games you end up with 24,239 unique questions. That’s a lot of questions! I wanted to get a sense of what kind of questions appeared in each game. So I did a process that is becoming my goto workflow of embedding -> clustering -> sample cluster members. I used Google’s embedding 2 model, did HDBSCAN and k-means clustering and used my local Qwen3.6-35B-A3B model to sample the members and categorize them. I UMAP’d them into this interface:

Finally I was thinking, out of 24K questions there HAD to be some wrong ones in there. I don’t mean question answer pairs that were correct in 1989 but wrong today I mean straight up incorrect. To find these I used a local LLM Qwen 3.6 35B A3B to scan 20 questions at a time to flag possible issues. The second pass then did a more thorough skeptical check that thinned down that previous pass and then finally sent those that kicked up as wrong in both passes to gemini-3.6-flash with google search grounding enabled. Then I reviewed the output of that last pass and found over 50 questions that were incorrect. Oftentimes the wrong questions are just an issue of the writer inverting the clue or other minor problem but sometimes boldly incorrect. Here is a list:

Update:

I compared the game clue dataset to one I found on Github (thanks to Reddit user RegisPhone for suggesting) that has 500K clues over the 42 seasons of the show with the goal to find the overlap.

Of the 24,240 unique game questions, 11,883 (49.0%) match an aired clue: 8,489 with identical normalized text (case, accents, punctuation and spelled-out numbers ignored) and 3,394 fuzzy (same answer, clue similarity ≥ 0.80 — mostly clues reworded or shortened to fit console screens).

The games cluster around three season spikes — seasons 1–2 (1984–86), season 5 (1988–89), and season 11 (1994–95) — so each game generation licensed a specific era’s clue bank: N64 (1998) matches 88% (mostly season 11), the Sega/SNES family ~67–72%, Game Boy/Game Gear ~56%, and the NES 25th Anniversary 40%. And NES rev1 (2.6%), Junior (2.0%), and all three Sports Editions (~1%) essentially never match and their questions appear to be custom-written rather than taken from aired episodes.
